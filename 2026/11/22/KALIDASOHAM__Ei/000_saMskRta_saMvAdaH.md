### KALIDASO’HAM (I AM KALIDASA)

#### SCENE 1

Setting: The King’s Grand Assembly Hall (Sabhamandap)

Time: Morning

(The King is seated on a royal throne studded with gold and precious gems. The entire assembly hall is redolent with the fragrance of sandalwood and aloe wood. The court is gathered with the state's most eminent scholars and Brahmins. The atmosphere is highly jubilant. The chanting of Vedic mantras and deep scriptural debates are underway. Amidst all these scholars, the venerable Royal Priest (Rajpurohit) is seated on a golden seat, appearing as the very embodiment of knowledge and penance. All the courtiers and the King himself hold him in the highest reverence.)

  

(Suddenly, from the main doors of the assembly hall, a sixteen-year-old maiden enters. A unique radiance glows on her face, and her gait reflects absolute confidence. She walks straight towards the royal throne. Without waiting for any formal protocol, she joyously embraces the King and sits on the lap of her affection-filled father. This is Vidyottama.)

  

(The King’s chest swells with pride. Looking at his daughter with deep paternal affection, he addresses the entire assembly.)

  

The King: (Smiling proudly) "O Venerable Guru! O Learned Scholars! Meet my daughter. Just today, she has returned from Kashi after completing her education. I have learned through correspondence that my young daughter has defeated the greatest, most formidable pundits and preceptors of Kashi in scriptural debates (Shastrartha). Witnessing her unfathomable knowledge and intellect, the scholarly society of Kashi has adorned her with the title of 'Vidyottama' (The Supreme in Knowledge)."

  

(Hearing this, a wave of joy and astonishment runs through the entire assembly. The Brahmins begin discussing among themselves with wonder. The Royal Priest's heart also fills with immense joy upon seeing such a brilliant maiden. Looking at Vidyottama with paternal affection and blessing, he recites a moral verse.)

  

Royal Priest: "Excellent! Excellent, O Daughter! This is a matter of supreme fortune for all of us. Listen, let me also impart some wisdom to you."

  

Vidyottama: (Thinking mockingly to herself) ‘Now he is going to teach me?’

  

Royal Priest:

prajāpatestu saṃkalpāt sambhūtā bhuvanapriyāḥ

amṛtaṃ brāhmaṇā gāvo rudrāśceti catuṣṭayam

\[English Translation: By the resolve of the Creator (Prajapati), these four, beloved to the worlds, were manifested: Nectar, Brahmins, Cows, and the Rudra deities.\]

  

"Thus, daughter, the significance that nectar holds in this creation is the same significance held by cows, Brahmins, and the Rudra deities."

  

(Hearing these words of the Royal Priest, the other Brahmins chant "Sadhu! Sadhu!" (Well said! Well said!) in agreement. However, a mocking smile floats on Vidyottama's lips. She rises from her place, looks straight into the Royal Priest's eyes, and speaks in a sarcastic tone—)

  

Vidyottama: "Forgive me, Priest! But this is a very low-grade thought. In reality, not just the cow, but every animal is a fragment of the Supreme Soul and is therefore praiseworthy. Your thought belongs to ordinary people engrossed in materialism.

  

The truth is that no one is a Brahmin or a Shudra by birth. As it has been said—"

śūdre suvṛttaṃ bhavellakṣyaṃ dvije tacca na vidyate

na vai śūdro bhavecchūdro brāhmaṇo na ca brāhmaṇaḥ

\[English Translation: If virtuous conduct is found in a Shudra, and that same conduct is absent in a twice-born (Brahmin), then that Shudra is no longer a Shudra, and that Brahmin is no longer a Brahmin.\]

  

(At this, a follower of the Royal Priest stands up.)

Follower Brahmin: "Princess! You should not speak in such a manner. As the scriptures say—"

brāhmaṇo janmanā śreyān sarveṣāṃ prāṇināmiha

tapasā vidyayā tuṣṭyā kimu harikalayā yutaḥ

\[English Translation: A Brahmin is superior by birth among all living beings in this world. Then what to speak of one who is endowed with penance, knowledge, contentment, or a fraction of Lord Vishnu's divine grace!\]

  

Vidyottama: "By birth? No one is a Brahmin or a Shudra by birth.

kulīnamakulīnaṃ vā vīraṃ puruṣamāninam

cāritrameva vyākhyāti śuciṃ vā yadi vā'śucim

\[English Translation: Whether one is of noble birth or low birth, a true hero or merely one who boasts of his masculinity, whether one is pure or impure—it is only one's character that defines them.\]

  

"And furthermore, Brahmins are notorious for their greed; they feast without hesitation even when someone dies!

alaṃkārapriyo viṣṇurjaladhārāpriyaḥ śivaḥ

namaskārapriyo bhānurbrāhmaṇo bhojanapriyaḥ

\[English Translation: Lord Vishnu loves ornaments, Lord Shiva loves the continuous pouring of water, the Sun God loves salutations, and a Brahmin loves food!\]

  

"I heard that just yesterday, the Royal Priest went to feast at the funeral rites (Shraddha) of a merchant's wife?"

(Hearing this grave insult hurled at the oldest and most revered Royal Priest by a sixteen-year-old maiden, the entire assembly is left stunned. A few young, angry Brahmins stand up from their seats.)

  

A Brahmin: (In a restrained but angry tone) "Princess! You cannot speak of the venerable Priest in this..."

  

(Before the Brahmin can finish his sentence, the Royal Priest raises his hand, signaling him to remain silent. The Royal Priest's gaze is fixed on the King. He was hoping that, as a father, the King would certainly reprimand his daughter for this arrogance and audacity. But the King is silent; his face still bears a faint smile mixed with pride in his daughter's scholarship. The King's silence pierces the Royal Priest's heart like an arrow.)

  

(The Royal Priest's face turns red with wrath and humiliation. He rises from his seat. There is an apocalyptic fire in his eyes. He takes water in his right hand from his ritual spoon (achamani), ties his Shikha (tuft of hair) into a tight, hard knot, and speaks in a voice that makes the entire assembly hall tremble—)

  

Royal Priest: "Silence, everyone! Where power becomes blind in the arrogance of knowledge, scriptures are not recited there; vows are taken! Listen, O King! Today, you have not insulted a Guru, but you have insulted the dignity of Knowledge itself. And hear this well... this is a Brahmin's vow!"

  

(Holding his tied Shikha, the Royal Priest declares)

"Until I grind your arrogant daughter's ego into the dust, until I shatter the pride of her false knowledge into pieces, I shall not untie this Shikha! And if I fail in this resolve, I shall cut off this Shikha forever, offer it at the feet of Mother Saraswati, and never bear a Shikha again for the rest of my life! Om!"

  

(Hearing this terrifying vow, a dead silence falls over the entire assembly. The King's face turns pale, and Vidyottama, too, is stunned for a moment.)

  

(Without wasting a single second, the Royal Priest turns towards the doors of the royal palace and departs swiftly. Wounded by this grave insult, three other radiant Brahmins present in the assembly rise from their seats. Staring directly at the King, they too tightly tie their respective Shikhas in support of the Royal Priest—as if adopting his vow as their own—and abandon the royal court, walking out as his followers.)

  

(A dreadful and ominous silence now sprawls across the assembly hall.)

  

(Scene Fades Out)

#### SCENE 2

Setting: A forest path outside the royal palace

Time: Afternoon

  

(Background setting: Daily affairs in the royal court continue as usual. The Royal Priest and his follower Brahmins still frequent the court, but the former radiance on their faces is gone. Meanwhile, Princess Vidyottama, staying away from the affairs of the court, remains busy studying scriptures and enjoying leisure with her friends in the inner chambers of the palace. She no longer visits the court. However, the fire of that day's grave insult continues to burn fiercely in the hearts of the Royal Priest and his three followers. They are rotting with anguish from within.)

  

(The scene begins. The Royal Priest is returning via a forest path along with his three follower Brahmins. Deep lines of worry mark all four faces.)

  

First Brahmin: (With despair and anger) "Gurudev! At this rate, our vow will remain unfulfilled. That arrogant princess doesn't even come to the royal court anymore. How on earth will we break her ego?"

  

Second Brahmin: "You speak the truth, brother! The pride of that girl's scholarship is so strong that defeating her in a scriptural debate is beyond the capacity of any ordinary pundit. We must find an extraordinary path."

  

(The Royal Priest is silent, walking slowly, lost in deep thought. Suddenly, the sound of an axe chopping— 'Thwack! Thwack!' — falls on their ears.)

  

(They all look up. There is a massive tree by the edge of the path. Sitting high up on one of its branches is a young man. He holds an axe in his hand, and with all his might, he is chopping the very branch on which he is sitting.)

  

(Witnessing this monumental stupidity, the Brahmins stop in their tracks.)

Third Brahmin: (Panicking and waving his hands) "Hey! Hey! Hey! You fool! What are you doing? If that branch falls, you will..."

  

(Seeing the radiant Brahmins standing below and hearing their voices, the woodcutter's attention shifts downward. Seeing revered Brahmins adorned in bark garments and sacred threads, his innate cultural respect awakens. To offer his salutations, the woodcutter stands up right on that half-cut branch. Just as he folds his hands and bows his head, his head smashes forcefully into a thick branch directly above him. He loses his balance, the half-cut branch snaps, and with a loud 'THUD!', the woodcutter falls straight to the ground.)

  

(As soon as he falls, the three fellow Brahmins cannot contain their laughter and burst into uproarious guffaws.)

  

(But the Royal Priest does not laugh. He stands perfectly still. His piercing gaze fixes on the face of the woodcutter lying on the ground. Covered in dust, the young man's face displays less pain and more of a boundless, innocent foolishness. Seeing him, a sudden flash of brilliance enters the Royal Priest's eyes—as if he had found a guiding light in the darkness.)

  

(The Royal Priest steps forward and asks the woodcutter to get up.)

Royal Priest: (Observing his face intently) "What is your name, young man?"

  

(The woodcutter stutters and begins to speak.)

Woodcutter: (Stuttering and scratching his head) "N... N... N... N... N... N... N... N... Name...?"

  

(Seeing this, the follower Brahmins are about to laugh again, but the Royal Priest stops them with a hand gesture. A mysterious, diplomatic smile floats onto the Priest's lips. He realizes that to chop down the ego of the great scholar Vidyottama, the 'axe' he needed was this absolute fool.)

  

Royal Priest: (In an extremely affectionate and sweet tone) "Let it be. What's in a name? You are suffering for no reason cutting wood in this forest. Come with us."

  

(The woodcutter stares at them, his eyes wide with surprise. Suddenly, an expression of helplessness appears in his eyes. Pointing towards his axe, he stammers—)

  

Woodcutter: "M... M... M... Mother is s... si... sick. I h... h... have to s... sell wood and t... t... take m... med... medicine th... there."

  

(Hearing this, the Royal Priest and his followers exchange glances. The woodcutter's helplessness becomes a golden opportunity for them.)

  

First Brahmin: (With a tone of slight pride and assurance) "Oh, you fool! How many physicians will you be able to afford by selling wood? Come with us."

  

Royal Priest: (In a very affectionate yet calculating tone) "Do not worry about your mother in the slightest, son! Do you not know who I am? I am the Royal Priest. There is such power in our mantras and blessings that, if we wish, even a lifeless corpse would start breathing! Your mother is merely sick, nothing will happen to her. By our glory and blessings, she will recover on her own."

  

(The woodcutter begins looking at the radiant Brahmins with awe and reverence.)

Royal Priest: (Tempting him further) "We will take you to the royal palace. There, you will receive so much wealth that you can buy whatever you want for your mother, and you will get fifty-six varieties of delicious delicacies to eat. All you have to do is this: you must not open your mouth in front of anyone. No matter what happens, you will remain completely silent. You will only do exactly as we tell you. Do you agree?"

  

(The innocent woodcutter, having placed full faith in the ascetic power of the Brahmins, and enticed by the thought of food and wealth, immediately nods his head in joyful agreement.)

  

(The Royal Priest casts a victorious glance at his followers. The three Brahmins now begin to understand what their Guru intends to do.)

  

(They manipulate the absolute fool of a woodcutter and take him along with them. Leaving his axe right there, the woodcutter follows them, driven by the hope of his mother's recovery and the greed for food.)

  

(Scene Fades Out)

SCENE 3

Setting: The Royal Assembly Hall and the Inner Chambers (Princess's Room)

Time: Morning

(The scene opens with the deep, resonant sounds of conch shells and kettle drums. The King and all the courtiers present in the assembly hall are looking toward the main doors in astonishment.)

(The Royal Priest and his three Brahmin disciples enter the assembly hall with an unprecedented, grand procession. The directions echo with the chanting of mantras—)

(Walking in the center of them is the woodcutter, who is now absolutely impossible to recognize. He has been given the appearance of a supremely accomplished, austere ascetic. He wears garments made of bark, large rosaries of Rudraksha beads adorn his neck and arms, and his forehead is marked with the three ash lines (Tripundra) of Lord Shiva. The most striking feature is a clean cloth tied securely over his mouth.)

(The woodcutter walks slowly and with profound seriousness, exactly as the Brahmins had instructed him (although the sheer panic and awe in his eyes upon seeing the grandeur of the royal palace is evident, which the Royal Priest cleverly masks).)

Royal Priest: (In a highly elevated and authoritative voice) "Attention, O King! Rise from your royal throne and offer full prostration to our supremely realized, omniscient, and most revered Gurudev!"

(Upon hearing this, the King immediately descends from his throne. Along with him, the entire royal assembly stands up. Without a moment's delay, the King lies flat on the ground, offering full prostration to the 'feigned-sage' (the woodcutter). All the other courtiers bow their heads in deep reverence and follow suit.)

The King: (With folded hands)  
dhanyo'haṃ kṛtakṛtyo'haṃ yadidaṃ sudinaṃ mama /  
āgatāḥ santo me gehe sākṣāt nārāyaṇaḥ svayam //  
\[English Translation: I am blessed, I am fulfilled, today is a highly auspicious day for me. A saint, the very embodiment of Lord Narayana himself, has arrived at my home.\]

(The King rises and, looking in wonder at the woodcutter's bound mouth, asks respectfully.)

The King: "O Royal Priest! My royal palace has been sanctified by the dust of this great Mahatma's feet. But... why has the Mahatma tied this cloth over his mouth?"

(At this, one of the Royal Priest's cunning Brahmin disciples steps forward.)

Brahmin Disciple: (Feigning utmost humility) "Your Majesty! He is our supreme Guru. Just today, he has returned after completing a severe penance in the caves of the Himalayas. He is currently observing a rigorous vow of silence. He will not utter a single word from his mouth. This cloth is a symbol of that strict vow and discipline."

The King: (Bowing in reverence) "Aho! Such formidable penance!"

Brahmin Disciple: "Yes, Your Majesty! While traveling here, he heard the widespread tales of your daughter Vidyottama's divine scholarship and her scriptural debates. Hearing this, the thirst for knowledge awakened within Gurudev, and he has come here with the desire to personally engage in a scriptural debate with the Princess."

The King: (With surprise and joy) "This is a matter of supreme fortune for us! But if the Mahatma is silent, how will the debate take place?"

Royal Priest: (Intervening) "The debate will take place in the language of signs and gestures, O King! Which I myself shall interpret for the assembly. But before this debate begins, the Mahatma has a condition, a resolve that you must listen to carefully."

The King: "Command me, Gurudev, what is this resolve?"

Royal Priest: (In a deep and challenging tone) "The resolve is this, O King... if the Mahatma is defeated by your daughter in this debate, he will forsake all his honor and prestige, touch your daughter's feet in this open assembly, shave off his ascetic tuft of hair (shikha), and return forever to the caves of the Himalayas, never to show his face to anyone again! But..."

(A pin-drop silence falls over the royal assembly.)

Royal Priest: "But! If the Mahatma emerges victorious... then your daughter Vidyottama will have to forsake all her scholarship and ego, touch his feet, and accept him as husband!"

(Hearing this extraordinary proposal, the King's eyes light up with joy.)

The King: (Overwhelmed with emotion) "I accept! If my daughter can be married to such a perfected and enlightened sage from the Himalayas, what greater fortune could there be for a father!"

(The King immediately orders a messenger to convey this entire account to the Princess.)

(The lighting on the stage shifts. The scene is now set in the inner chambers, where Vidyottama is seated with her friends.)

(The messenger arrives panting and narrates the entire account of the royal court, the silent Mahatma, and the strict conditions of the debate in detail to the Princess.)

(Hearing all this, no lines of fear or worry emerge on Vidyottama's face; rather, her eyes gleam with excitement and the frenzy of certain victory. She is highly amused.)

Vidyottama: (Laughing joyously) "A scriptural debate! And that too through signs? Ha ha ha! Excellent!"

(In her elation, she slips a precious ring from her finger and tosses it toward the messenger as a reward.)

Vidyottama: (Smiling, but in an arrogant tone) "You have brought very auspicious news today, messenger! Go, tell father that this challenge is completely acceptable to me! Today, a highly excellent opportunity has presented itself... after dealing with the Brahmins of this state, it is time to shatter the pride of another fraud who has come down from the Himalayas!"

(Vidyottama's face wears a proud and victorious smile. The messenger bows his head and departs.)

(Scene Fades Out)

#### SCENE 4

Setting: The Royal Assembly Hall

Time: Afternoon

(The assembly hall is packed to the brim. Seated in the center on a high, magnificent throne is the 'feigned-Mahatma' (the woodcutter), observing his vow of silence. The Royal Priest and his Brahmin disciples are seated around him with absolute solemnity.)

(Suddenly, the kettle drums sound. Princess Vidyottama enters with her friends and takes her place at the elevated viewing balcony reserved for royal women. Her face radiates the pride of fathomless scholarship. Sitting in the assembly below, the woodcutter stares wide-eyed at the breathtakingly beautiful woman; he has absolutely no idea what is about to happen here.)

Royal Priest: (Making the announcement) "Let the scriptural debate commence! Princess, please present your first proposition."

(Vidyottama looks down from the balcony. With supreme confidence, she establishes her first philosophical doctrine in Sanskrit, and while speaking, she raises her hand and shows all five fingers toward the Mahatma.)

Vidyottama:  
pañcamahābhūtairnirmitamidaṃ sarvaṃ jagat   
kṣitijalapāvakagaganasamīrā eva paramārthaḥ   
\[English Translation: This entire universe is created from the five great elements. Earth, Water, Fire, Space, and Air—these five elements alone are the ultimate truth.\]

(The woodcutter sitting below does not understand a single word of Sanskrit. He only sees Vidyottama's five raised fingers. His foolish and naive mind thinks— "Hey! Who is she to threaten to slap me in an open assembly? If she slaps me, I will punch her!")

(Thinking this, the woodcutter feigns anger and tightly clenches his fist, showing his fist threateningly toward Vidyottama.)

(Seeing this deeply unrefined gesture, a stunned silence falls over the assembly. Vidyottama's eyebrows knit in a frown. Before the King can ask anything, the Royal Priest immediately stands up and, with a triumphant smile, translates the philosophical meaning of the gesture.)

Royal Priest: "Excellent! Supremely excellent! Your Majesty, the Princess stated that the universe is made of five elements. But the answer our Gurudev has given by showing a fist is unimaginable! Gurudev is saying—

pṛthak pañcatattvāni niṣprayojanāni, militvā eva sṛṣṭinirmāṇaṃ kurvanti /  
\[English Translation: Separately, these five elements are purposeless. Just as the five fingers must come together to form a fist, similarly, only when these five elements unite together do they create this universe.\]"

(Hearing this magnificent interpretation, the entire assembly erupts in cheers of "Sadho! Sadho!" (Well said! Well said!). Vidyottama is left astounded. She had not imagined even in her dreams that any sage could cut through her doctrine with such simplicity.)

(She composes herself and prepares to establish her second, most irrefutable proposition. This time, she takes refuge in the philosophy of Advaita Vedanta (Non-dualism). She raises one finger and points it toward the Mahatma.)

Vidyottama:  
ekamevādvitīyaṃ brahma neha dvitīyaṃ kiñcana  
tattvamasi nāsti dvaitasyāvakāśaḥ   
\[English Translation: That Supreme Brahman is only one, there is no second to Him. There is no multiplicity here at all. All is one; there is absolutely no room for duality.\]

(The woodcutter, once again, understands no Sanskrit. He only sees Vidyottama's one raised finger and thinks— "Oh! Now she is after my eyes. She is threatening to poke one of my eyes out? If you poke out my one eye, I will poke out both of your eyes!")

(The woodcutter immediately pulls out two fingers (index and middle) and aggressively thrusts them toward Vidyottama.)

(Vidyottama is paralyzed by this crude gesture. But the Royal Priest instantly stands up to interpret it with the demeanor of an omniscient scholar.)

Royal Priest: "Astounding! Inconceivable! Your Majesty! The Princess presented the doctrine of Advaita, stating that Brahman is one. But by showing two fingers, Gurudev has proven the most profound mystery of creation. Guruji is saying—

satyaṃ brahmaikaṃ parantu sṛṣṭivyavahārārthaṃ dvaitaṃ sarvatra nihitam!  
\[English Translation: Although Brahman is indeed one, for the operation and functioning of this universe, duality (the existence of two) is inevitably present everywhere.\]

Royal Priest: (Explaining further) "Just like day and night, mother and father, truth and untruth, inhalation and exhalation, and even Prakriti (Nature) and Purusha (Consciousness)! It is duality that manifests that one Brahman. Where is the universe without the union of Shiva and Shakti?"

(Hearing this interpretation, the ground seems to slip from beneath Vidyottama's feet. Her entire edifice of scholarship collapses. Drops of sweat break out on her forehead due to fear, humiliation, and sheer astonishment. She wants to speak further, but her throat has gone dry. Her lips tremble, but not a single word comes out. She has been left completely speechless.)

(Just then, a Brahmin disciple of the Royal Priest speaks up in a loud voice—)

Brahmin Disciple: "Members of the assembly! It is clearly evident that Princess Vidyottama has been left without an answer. She must now accept her defeat, come down according to the vow, and touch the feet of our Gurudev, this great Mahatma!"

(All the Brahmins present in the assembly declare in unison— "Sadho! Sadho! Victory to the Mahatma!")

(Vidyottama's entire ego has shattered. Standing by the balcony window, she breaks down and begins to weep bitterly.)

(The King, who on one hand is saddened by his daughter's defeat, but on the other hand is overjoyed to attain such a highly realized sage as a son-in-law, calls her to his side.)

The King: (Affectionately stroking Vidyottama's head) "Do not cry, daughter! There is no shame in this. To debate with such an omniscient sage who has come directly from the Himalayas, and to hold your ground for even two questions, is a massive achievement in itself. Go, go and touch his feet, and according to the vow, accept him as your husband. We shall have you married to him at the earliest."

(Weeping, with trembling legs, Vidyottama descends the stairs and enters the assembly hall. Forsaking all her pride, she falls to her knees and touches the feet of the woodcutter.)

(The Royal Priest's heart fills with the jubilation of victory. He steps forward, holds Vidyottama by the shoulders, lifts her, and places her delicate hand into the rough hand of the feigned-Mahatma (the woodcutter).)

(At that very moment, musical instruments begin to play.)

(From the upper balcony, the royal women joyously shower flower petals. The Royal Priest and the other Brahmins begin to sing auspicious hymns in a loud voice with Vedic mantras—)

maṅgalaṃ bhagavān viṣṇurmaṅgalaṃ garuḍadhvajaḥ  
maṅgalaṃ puṇḍarīkākṣo maṅgalāyatano hariḥ

(The royal assembly drowns in celebration.)

(That very week, in the grandest ceremony of the state, with the sacred fire as witness, the marriage of the supremely learned Vidyottama to the absolute fool of a woodcutter is solemnized with complete pomp and royal splendor.)

(Scene Fades Out)

SCENE 5

Setting: The Inner Chambers (Vidyottama’s decorated bedchamber) and the King’s Chamber

Time: Night (The first night of marriage)

(The scene begins.)

(The bedchamber is glowing with the light of oil lamps and the fragrance of fresh flowers. On a bed of flowers, the great scholar Vidyottama, now a bride, sits draped in red silk, her face covered with a veil, waiting for her 'supremely wise' husband.)

(Outside the chamber, the Brahmin disciples of the Royal Priest bring the woodcutter (who is now dressed as a groom). They push him inside the room and bolt the heavy doors from the outside.)

(The woodcutter steps into the room. He looks around in sheer wonder and then his eyes fall upon the breathtakingly beautiful Vidyottama seated on the bed. Seeing the Princess in this form, he becomes completely infatuated. He walks slowly toward his wife and, with trembling hands, gently lifts her veil.)

(Vidyottama sits with her eyes lowered in modesty and reverence, expecting a Sanskrit verse or a Vedic greeting from her husband.)

(Just then, the woodcutter's gaze wanders out the large window of the bedchamber.)

(Outside, camels are walking along the royal path. Seeing a camel, the woodcutter, out of his innate ignorance and childish nature, becomes overly excited. Forgetting the Princess, he points his finger toward the window and yells in an impure, unrefined pronunciation—)

Woodcutter: "Uṭra! Uṭra!"

(The correct Sanskrit word for camel is 'uṣṭra', but due to his absolute ignorance, he was shouting 'uṭra'.)

(Hearing this impure and foolish pronunciation, Vidyottama's ears perk up. Such a gross grammatical error from the mouth of an 'enlightened sage returned from the Himalayas'? She jerks her head up in shock.)

(The woodcutter turns back toward Vidyottama. Mesmerized by her beauty, he begins to stutter—)

Woodcutter: "P... Prin... Prin... Princess!"

(Vidyottama springs up from her place. A mixed shadow of suspicion and dread clouds her eyes.)

Vidyottama: (In a sharp, piercing tone) "What is all this? Who are you? Speak the truth!"

(The woodcutter panics. Stuttering, he spills the truth—)

Woodcutter: "Me? M... me... wood... woodcutter."

(Hearing this, the ground slips from beneath Vidyottama's feet. The woodcutter, enchanted by her beauty, ignorantly steps forward to embrace her. But Vidyottama, filled with disgust and fury, shoves him away with all her might. She runs to the chamber door, only to find it bolted from the outside.)

Vidyottama: (Hissing in wrath) "You fool! Illiterate! Fraud! Get out of my sight. How dare you even think of touching me?"

(Terrified, the woodcutter immediately falls at Vidyottama's feet.)

Woodcutter: (Weeping and begging) "I... husband... Do not... abandon... Priest... said... I did... Brahmins... worthy… of worship."

(Vidyottama now understands the entire conspiracy. Her unfathomable scholarship and immense ego have been polluted today by marrying a mere woodcutter. She goes mad with rage and delivers a vicious kick to the woodcutter lying at her feet.)

Vidyottama: "Get out of here this very instant! Do not dare to touch me!"

(The woodcutter begins to wail. Out of utter helplessness and sheer foolishness, he gets up and steps forward to embrace Vidyottama once again.)

(This time, Vidyottama's wrath crosses all boundaries. She, resembling the fierce Devi Caṇḍī, strikes the woodcutter with a violent kick. He loses his balance and falls straight out of the large window, tumbling down onto the royal path below. Fortunately, the height is short, and he survives.)

(Vidyottama peers down from the window and hurls curses at him with absolute disgust.)

Vidyottama: "Get away from here! Never show me this foolish face of yours again, you lowly wretch!"

(Weeping from fear, pain, and humiliation, the woodcutter disappears into the darkness, never looking back.)

(Weeping bitterly, Vidyottama bangs on the chamber door with all her strength. After a while, a maid comes running and opens the door. Seeing Vidyottama's terrifying face, red with rage, the maid trembles.)

(With eyes bloodshot from anger and crying, Vidyottama goes straight into the King's chamber. Weeping, she narrates the entire account and the Brahmins' horrifying deceit to her father.)

(The King's heart is shattered upon hearing this. He immediately summons the Royal Priest.)

(The Royal Priest enters the King's chamber along with his three Brahmin disciples. Today, there is no respect for the King on their faces; instead, there is the arrogance of a terrifying victory.)

(The King is about to say something, but the Royal Priest raises his hand, stopping him. He unties the knot of his śikhā (tuft of hair), and letting his unfastened hair fly, speaks in an apocalyptic tone—)

Royal Priest: "Say nothing, O King! Today, that vow of ours has been fulfilled, the one we took in your packed royal assembly! Your arrogant daughter kept insulting us, our scriptures, and the dignity of knowledge in the open court, while you sat in the intoxication of power and watched in silence!"

(The Royal Priest's voice echoes off the walls of the chamber. The other three disciples also untie their śikhās.)

Royal Priest: "Therefore, we married off your scholarly daughter to an illiterate, absolute fool of a woodcutter and fulfilled our resolve. Go, O King! Now watch this daughter of yours, polluted by this marriage, weep inch by inch before your eyes for the rest of her life... exactly as you watched our humiliation!"

(Having delivered this crushing lightning bolt, the Royal Priest and his disciples turn and leave the chamber without a backward glance.)

(A dreadful silence now fills the room. Vidyottama runs, clings to her father's neck, and begins to wail uncontrollably.)

(The King sits on his throne, paralyzed like a statue of stone. His hand rests on the back of his weeping daughter. His gaze is fixed on the emptiness ahead. He is a patient and brave King, but before this immense helplessness of a father and the cruel strike of destiny, his patience shatters.)

(Staring into that boundless emptiness, a single teardrop spills from the King's right eye and falls onto his cheek.)

(Scene Fades Out)

#### SCENE 6

Setting: The Woodcutter's Hut, a forest path, and the Temple of Mother Kālī on a hill

Time: Midnight

(The scene begins.)

(Humiliated by the royal palace, weeping bitterly, and bruised, the woodcutter runs all the way back and reaches his dilapidated hut.)

(Crying, he enters the hut, but the moment he sees the sight inside, the scream dies in his throat. On the cot in front of him lies the lifeless body of his sick mother. Due to the illness and death, her body has turned completely blue. There is no medicine in the hut, not a single grain of food, and there was no one to even offer a drop of water to the unfortunate woman in her final moments.)

(The words of the Royal Priest echo in the woodcutter's ears— "Nothing will happen to your mother, by our ascetic power and blessings she will recover.")

(His naive mind cannot bear the trauma of this cruel deceit and his mother's death. He faints right at the doorway, falling with a thud.)

(After sometime, he wakes up. He crawls to his mother's corpse, clings to it, and begins to wail and scream. There is so much agony in his screams that his voice cracks and tears from crying. He wails in despair and unable to bear the excruciating grief, faints once again.)

(The stage lighting dims and then brightens after a few moments, indicating the passage of time.)

(A while later, the woodcutter opens his eyes again. But this time, as he rises, there is no sorrow on his face, no pain, no happiness, and no resentment. A terrifying apathy has taken over his eyes—a numbness deeper than death itself.)

(He rises mechanically. He lifts his mother's blue corpse and respectfully lays it on the ground. Then, displaying absolutely no emotion, he breaks the old wooden cot with his bare hands. He places the broken wood over his mother's body. He burns an oil lamp from the corner of the hut, sets the structure on fire, and turns the dry hut itself into her funeral pyre.)

(Before his eyes, the entire hut, along with his mother's body, is engulfed in roaring flames. The flames touch the night sky.)

(The woodcutter turns his back to this apocalyptic fire and walks out into the night. He does not look back even once.)

(The background music becomes extremely slow and melancholic.)

(He has no sense of where he is going. He is just walking into the darkness. His feet land aimlessly. Sometimes he steps in cow dung, sometimes he falls into muddy puddles. The thorns of the forest tear at his legs and body, and he gets drenched in muck. He falls, and without a single groan, mechanically rises and keeps walking forward.)

(Walking endlessly, unknowingly, his feet enter the ancient temple of Mother Kālī, situated high on a hill.)

(Inside the sanctum sanctorum, only a constant oil lamp is burning. The woodcutter stumbles into the sanctum. He lifts his head in the emptiness, and his eyes fall directly upon the face of the massive, terrifying, yet deeply compassionate idol of Mother Kālī.)

(The moment he looks at the face of the mother-incarnate Devi, the dam inside the woodcutter—the one he had held back with sheer numbness—breaks in a terrifying flood.)

(He falls to the floor, grabbing the idol's feet, and begins to weep bitterly. And in this ultimate state of grief, a miracle occurs— his stuttering vanishes completely! The agony has burned away the blockage in his throat. In a cracked, heavy, and choked voice, he questions the Mother of the Universe directly—)

Woodcutter: (Crying, stepping back and spreading his arms) "What was my fault, Mother? What was my fault? I chopped wood from the forest, and with that, I served my mother. I never spread my hands to beg from anyone in my entire life. I obeyed those Brahmins only because I considered them equal to gods and worthy of worship!"

(He crawls on his knees, moving closer to the idol.)

Woodcutter: "I do not have much knowledge, Bhavānī... but since childhood, my mother taught me only this: to always respect Brahmins and obey their commands. I thought, if I listen to them, by their grace, my sick mother would recover. That is why, whatever they told me to do, I did it exactly. But... that Princess kicked me with disgust and threw me out of the window!"

(His tears begin to soak the floor of the temple. His voice now carries an intense agony mixed with the righteous rage of the unlettered.)

Woodcutter: "If I am uneducated, how is that my fault? Do uneducated people not have the right to have a wife? Do the mothers of uneducated people not have the right to live? I had no one in this world... except one mother... and she too died because of me! Because I was a fool. I thought the Royal Priest would cure my mother with his ascetic power. If I had even a fraction of intellect, if I could understand that this was all a lie, I would have never gone to that royal palace! I would have stayed with my mother; I would have served her!"

(He screams, and his scream echoes off the temple walls.)

Woodcutter: "Why didn't you give me this intellect? You are the Mother of the Universe, aren't you? You are Bhagavatī! Then why did you make me such a fool?"

(He enters a state of absolute frenzy. He goes right up to the stone feet of Mother Kālī's idol and begins to forcefully smash his forehead against those stone feet.)

Woodcutter: (Screaming with every strike of his head) "Why didn't you give it to me? Why didn't you give it to me? Why didn't you give it to me!"

('Thwack! Thwack! Thwack!' The sound of his skull hitting the stone echoes throughout the temple. In a few moments, the skin of his forehead splits open, and a thick stream of blood gushes out, turning the feet of Mother Kālī red.)

(He strikes one final blow—) "Why didn't you give it, Mother..." (And then his body goes entirely limp.)

(Drenched in blood, the great fool of a woodcutter falls unconscious at the feet of Mother Kālī. The stream of blood flowing from his forehead continuously bathes the feet of the deity. In the temple, only the sound of the flickering oil lamp remains.)

(Scene Fades Out)

#### SCENE 7

Setting: The Temple of Mother Kālī and an unfamiliar room

Time: Nighttime

(The scene begins.)

(A few drops of water suddenly fall on the closed eyes of the woodcutter, who is lying unconscious in the sanctum sanctorum of the temple. With the touch of those drops, he slowly opens his heavy eyelids.)

(He realizes that his head is no longer resting on the hard stone of the temple floor, but rather in the tender lap of a divine, dark-complexioned woman radiating an otherworldly glow. The divine woman is applying a paste of turmeric on the deep wound on his forehead with her own hands.)

(The woodcutter looks at her face in sheer awe. The Devi's eyes are blood-red with terrifying wrath, and due to the intensity of that very wrath, tears are streaming down from her eyes, falling as drops of water onto the woodcutter's face. Yet, even on that wrathful face, there is a supernatural maternal love and affection for the woodcutter. She is applying the turmeric paste with extreme tenderness.)

(A faint, trembling word escapes the woodcutter's mouth—)

Woodcutter: "Mother?"

Mother Kālī: (With red, wrathful eyes, but in a piercing, protective tone) "Do not worry in the slightest, my son! Whatever be the suffering you have had to endure until today, you shall not have to endure anything more."

(The woodcutter remains spellbound, listening to her.)

Mother Kālī: "In your previous birth, you were my big worshipper and devotee, but out of ignorance, you insulted a great ascetic and received his curse. Due to the effect of that curse, even though being born into a Brahmin lineage, you had to become a woodcutter in this life and endure such grave humiliation. But now, the duration of that curse has been completed."

(The wrath in Devi's eyes now becomes even more apocalyptic.)

Mother Kālī: "Taking advantage of your innocence, the arrogant Royal Priest who committed such a despicable deceit against you... I shall give him such a horrifying death that the entire royal assembly will tremble merely upon hearing of his fate! I am immensely pleased by your pure and innocent devotion."

(She affectionately runs her hand over the woodcutter's forehead.)

Mother Kālī: "I grant you this boon: a great poet like you would not exist in this world at present, nor shall there ever be one in the future. I grant you not only the memories of your past lives but also 'Self-Realization'. Whatever may be the delay that has happened until today but now it is over; from now on, your glory will spread across all ten directions, and the immortal works you write will be read for centuries to come. From today onwards, you shall be known in this world by the name 'Kālikeya Śarmā'."

(The moment he hears this boon, an infinite light of knowledge erupts within the woodcutter. All the layers of his foolishness burn to ashes. Becoming fully self-realized, he immediately rises from Devi's lap and sits up. With folded hands, he says with utmost humility—)

Woodcutter (The Newly Enlightened): "No, Mother! By receiving such a heavy name as 'Kālikeya Śarmā' and such fathomless knowledge, the ego of scholarship will once again take root within me. My name should only be 'Kālidāsa' (The servant of Kālī), because for my entire life, I only wish to remain your humble servant."

(Mother Kālī is captivated by this egolessness of her devotee. She swiftly places her hand on his head.)

Mother Kālī: "Tathāstu! (So be it!)"

(The embodiment of Speech, Vāgdevī (Sarasvatī), physically manifests within the throat of Kālidāsa. Sitting right there with folded hands, in a supremely sweet, rhythmic, and flawless Sanskrit, he begins to sing the highly renowned hymn of the devi, the 'Śyāmalā Daṇḍakam'—)

(Hearing this divine and flawlessly fluent hymn flowing from Kālidāsa's mouth, Mother Kālī is exceedingly pleased. Suddenly, a blinding, white, and divine radiance—equal to the light of a billion suns—bursts forth from her divine form, blinding all directions.)

(An intense white light covers the stage, and the scene changes.)

(With this blinding light, Kālidāsa's divine dream shatters.)

(He jerks his eyes open. He sees that he is no longer on the hard floor of the temple, but rather lying on an unfamiliar and comfortable bed.)

(He sits up in a daze and touches his forehead. A thick paste of turmeric is still applied to his forehead, but to his absolute astonishment, the deep, fatal wound from striking the temple stone has completely healed!)

(This was no ordinary dream; this was the awakening of a great poet. Remembering the dream he just saw, Kālidāsa is filled with sheer awe.)

(Scene Fades Out)

#### SCENE 8

Setting: The house of the Head Priest of Mother Kālī's temple

Time: After dawn (Morning)

(The scene begins.)

(As Kālidāsa sits up on his bed, his gaze falls on the open doorway ahead. An elderly, motherly woman is standing at the door, discussing a serious matter with a man standing outside. Kālidāsa cannot hear their conversation clearly, but he can see expressions of deep shock and sorrow on the old woman's face. She shudders as she listens.)

(The man leaves. Cupping her cheeks in sorrow and disbelief, the old woman mutters, "Rāma... Rāma... Rāma..." and enters the room.)

(Just then, her eyes fall upon Kālidāsa sitting on the bed. A motherly joy spreads across her face.)

Old Woman: (Approaching affectionately) "Son! You are awake?"

(After attaining supreme knowledge, the very first words to spontaneously emerge from Kālidāsa's mouth are in the divine language, Sanskrit—beginning with the word 'Amba' (Mother). He asks with utmost humility—)

Kālidāsa: amba kutrāhaṃ. kimetat.  
\[English Translation: Mother! Where am I? What is all this?\]

(Hearing such flawless Sanskrit from Kālidāsa's mouth, the old woman smiles and, stroking his head, replies.)

Old Woman: "Son! I do not know how to speak Sanskrit that well, but having spent my life in the service of my husband, I understand it perfectly well. You are safe, son. My husband is the Head Priest of Mother Kālī's temple. The night you suffered that horrific injury, my husband had a dream that Mother Kālī herself was calling him. He ran to the temple in a panic and saw you lying unconscious at the feet of the deity, covered in mud and thorns, with blood flowing from your forehead."

(The old woman pauses for a moment and offers Kālidāsa some water to drink.)

Old Woman: "With the help of others, he immediately brought you here to our home and called for the royal physician. The physician treated you and said you had suffered a very deep trauma. If there had been any delay in bringing you, your life would have been in grave danger. Since then, I have been nursing you. Today, after many days... it is only by the boundless grace of Mother Kālī that you have opened your eyes."

(Seeing such selfless, motherly love and service in the heart of an unfamiliar old woman, Kālidāsa's heart swells with emotion. By the grace of the deity of Speech, he immediately composes a verse in praise of the old woman while sitting there.)

(Reciting that verse, Kālidāsa steps down from the bed and offers full prostration to the old woman.)

(The old woman is left astounded. With motherly affection, she lifts him up and seats him back on the bed.)

Old Woman: "Son! You seem to be a very great scholar! Even my husband doesn't speak such fluent and beautiful Sanskrit. Truly, the Devi has bestowed a great blessing upon you."

Kālidāsa: (Nodding his head with extreme humility) "Satyam... Satyam." (It is the truth... It is the truth.)

(Just then, Kālidāsa remembers the man at the door. He asks the old woman who the man was and what he was saying.)

(The old woman's face turns pale again. She explains—)

Old Woman: "Son, the very night those people brought you here in that condition, a horrifying incident took place in the royal palace. The Royal Priest of our state suddenly suffered a stroke of paralysis. His entire body turned to stone."

(Before Kālidāsa's eyes, the apocalyptic words of Mother Kālī echo— "I shall give that priest such a death...")

Old Woman: (Continuing in a trembling voice) "His disciples were nursing him day and night. But just now, that man brought the news that late last night, when the attendants and disciples were not in the room, a terrifying fire inexplicably broke out in the Royal Priest's chamber! The pundits estimate that, even in his paralyzed state, seeing the fire, he tried to flee from his bed and fell to the floor... and that heavy bed overturned and fell right on top of him!"

(The old woman shuts her eyes in horror.)

Old Woman: "It is believed, son... that due to the paralysis, he couldn't even scream for help, and while still alive... he was burned to ashes, agonizing under that very bed! Hearing the news of such an agonizing death of their father-like guru, his three main disciples have abandoned the royal court in sheer terror and have decided to take renunciation! Hearing this gave me goosebumps, son. May the Devi never give such a horrifying death even to an enemy!"

(Hearing this, Kālidāsa is left speechless. He realizes how irrefutable the fruits of karma and the punishment of the Devi truly are. Sitting on the bed, he folds both his hands, closes his eyes, and merely utters—)

Kālidāsa: (With deep reverence and awe) "Kālī!... Kālī!"

(A profound spiritual silence blankets the stage.)

(That day, Kālidāsa accepted food from the hands of that old woman, as if he were eating from the hands of his own mother. At the affectionate request of the elderly couple (the priest and his wife), Kālidāsa rested at their home for a few more days to recover completely.)

(When Kālidāsa was fully healed, he asked for their leave.)

(The elderly couple bid him farewell as if he were their own son. They adorned him with the most beautiful, clean, and excellent garments and some ornaments as best as they could. He was no longer a destitute, foolish woodcutter, but appeared as a radiant, self-realized, and divinely handsome great poet. Touching the feet of the elderly couple and taking their blessings, Kālidāsa sets out on a new journey.)

(Scene Fades Out)

#### SCENE 9 (FINAL SCENE)

Setting: The Royal Palace Inner Chambers (Vidyottama’s Room) and a subsequent time jump

Time: Daytime

(The scene begins.)

(Kālidāsa, who has now transformed from an ignorant woodcutter into a veritable sun of knowledge, marches straight toward the inner chambers of the royal palace. There is an unprecedented radiance on his face and a fearless, lion-like stride in his gait.)

(The guards stationed at the main gates of the inner chambers, seeing him as a stranger, cross their spears to block his path.)

(Kālidāsa stops. There is a fierce brilliance in his eyes. He speaks to the guards in an extremely sharp and authoritative tone—)

Kālidāsa: "Do you not know the punishment for blocking the path of the King's son-in-law?"

(Hearing that radiant and commanding voice, the guards are terrified. They lower their spears and clear the path. In this manner, silencing the other guards along the way with his sheer presence, Kālidāsa reaches straight into Vidyottama's chamber.)

(Inside the chamber, Vidyottama is conversing with her friends. Suddenly, seeing Kālidāsa standing at the door, all the friends are left stunned. Seeing a man confidently enter the private inner chambers, the friends step aside out of modesty.)

(Vidyottama recognizes him, and true to her nature, she asks him in a mocking tone—)

Vidyottama: "asti kaścid vāgviśeṣaḥ?"  
\[English Translation: Is there now any distinction in your speech (scholarship)?\]

(She rises from her seat and says with disdain) "Or have you come to deceive me again by putting on this disguise? Is there no grain left in your house to eat, that you have created this charade of a Brahmin to beg for alms? This is a fine illusion to trick the royal family! Who let you inside? Have you forgotten my kick from that night, that you have shown up here again with your face raised?"

(Vidyottama's tone becomes even more bitter) "The Royal Priest suffered the fate of his own deceit and was burned alive. Do you wish to meet the same fate, that you are inviting your own death by standing before me?"

(Kālidāsa, in a deep, composed voice, speaks in fluent divine language (Sanskrit)—)

Kālidāsa: "śrūyatāṃ devi! (Listen, O lady!)

bhartā tu khalu nārīṇāṃ guṇavānnirguṇo'pi vā  
dharmaṃ vimṛśamānānāṃ pratyakṣaṃ devi daivatam  
\[English Translation: O Queen! For women who reflect upon Dharma, their husband—whether endowed with virtues or devoid of them—is indeed their visible deity.\]

(This flawless and divine Sanskrit verse, uttered from Kālidāsa's mouth, strikes Vidyottama's ears like a thunderbolt.)

Kālidāsa: (In a sharp yet restrained tone) "Did your guru not even teach you this verse from Maharishi Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa? You abandoned me because you considered me a fool. The truth is, on that first night of our marriage, I was completely captivated just by looking at your unparalleled beauty; I wanted to offer you my love. But... you kicked your own husband with your feet and threw him out of the window!"

(Kālidāsa continues) "I thought that by enduring the Royal Priest's revenge, your intellect might have humbled a little. But what sins can the innate arrogance of a Kshatriya woman and the pride of scholarship not make a person commit? Little did I know that you are like a golden chalice filled with poison... beautiful only on the outside!"

(Vidyottama is listening to him, completely speechless.)

Kālidāsa: "I was indeed an ignorant woodcutter, but I was born into a Brahmin lineage. What do you think? Will your father, the King of this state, commit the sin of 'Brahmahatya' (killing a Brahmin) by executing me, just to satisfy your ego? And as for alms... do not even speak of alms, Princess! If you offer even a single grain to a Brahmin with respect, he will be satisfied. But the wealth of Kubera, if offered with insult, is unacceptable to us!"

(A dead silence blankets the chamber. Kālidāsa delivers his final verdict—)

Kālidāsa: "Now listen! I abandon you, right here, this very moment! And the answer to your question— 'asti kaścid vāgviśeṣaḥ'— will not be given by my tongue, but by my hands!"

(Saying this, Kālidāsa turns around and departs from the chamber.)

(As he turns, it is clearly visible that his eyes are not red with anger, but red with a deep, profound pain. His eyes are brimming with tears, and his lips are trembling. Deep inside, he had truly loved Vidyottama, and abandoning her was heartbreaking for him as well.)

(With Kālidāsa gone, Vidyottama remains standing there, paralyzed like a painted picture. She cannot comprehend whether she is witnessing a dream or if this is all reality.)

('The man who called a camel (uṣṭra) an 'uṭra', the man who could not string together a single proper sentence... that very man, today, taught me the lessons of ethics in fluent Sanskrit, shattered the pride of my scholarship, and on the contrary, abandoned me and walked away?')

(Her friends come near, shaking her and asking what happened, but she merely stands there like a statue of stone, staring into the emptiness.)

#### EPILOGUE

(The background music denoting the cycle of time plays on the stage.)

Voiceover: "That day, Kālidāsa abandoned that kingdom forever. He journeyed to the kingdom of the glorious Emperor Vikramāditya of Ujjayinī, a realm renowned throughout Āryāvarta for honoring poets. There, on the strength of his divine genius, Kālidāsa attained the most revered position (chief among the Nine Jewels) in the royal assembly. He continually enchanted the court with his profound scholarship and astonishing poetic mastery."

"Kālidāsa made the three words of the mockery uttered by Vidyottama— 'asti kaścid vāgviśeṣaḥ' — the ultimate goal of his life. He proved his wife's statement true, and with his pen, he composed three such epic masterpieces that immortalized him in the pages of history forever:"

1.  With the word 'asti', he composed the epic Kumārasambhavam—  
    (astyuttarasyāṃ diśi devatātmā...)

2.  With the word 'kaścid', he composed the lyrical poem Meghadūtam—  
    (kaścit kāntāvirahaguruṇā...)

3.  With the word 'vāg', he composed the epic Raghuvaṃśam—  
    (vāgarthāviva sampṛktau...)

"By the infallible boon of Mother Kālī and the grace of Mother Sarasvatī, his glory spread in all directions. To this day, no poet has ever been able to equal him. In Sanskrit literature, it is famously said that when scholars began counting the greatest poets, they took the name 'Kālidāsa' first and placed him on the 'Kaniṣṭhikā' (the little finger). But when no other poet could be found to equal him, the finger next to the Kaniṣṭhikā remained nameless, and that is why, to this day, that finger is called 'Anāmikā' (the nameless one)."

(On the stage, the grand, radiant idols of Mother Kālī and Vāgdevī (Sarasvatī) emerge, and Kālidāsa is seen sitting at their feet, writing on a manuscript.)

Final text (appears written on the screen/backdrop):  
"To the manifest Mother Kālī and her inseparable form Mother Vāgdevī, who transformed that ignorant woodcutter into 'Kavikulaguru Kālidāsa' (The Preceptor of the Family of Poets), this work is wholly dedicated."

(With the blowing of the conch shell and the singing of an auspicious hymn, the scene concludes.)
